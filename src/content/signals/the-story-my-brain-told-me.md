title: "The Story My Brain Told Me"
date: "26 September 2026"
category: "Psychology & Self-Awareness"
description: "Daniel Kahneman taught me that my own brain isn't always a reliable narrator. This is what I learned when I believed it anyway."
---


I have recently been reading Daniel Kahneman, which is an oddly unsettling experience when you've spent a few years learning that the voice in your head isn't the impartial narrator you assumed it was.

The brain is a remarkable piece of machinery. It detects patterns. It fills gaps. It makes predictions. It turns fragments into stories and occasionally, with the confidence of a witness who wasn't actually there.

I know this because, for a while, my brain told me a story about someone's intentions. And I believed it.
Not immediately. That's what makes these things difficult.

There were fragments of earlier experiences, uncertainty, things I thought I'd observed and understood. My circumstances weren't exactly operating at factory settings either. There was a lot happening at once, and I was trying to hold things together while finding my footing again.

Somewhere in all of that, my brain began connecting dots. The problem was that some of those dots didn't belong to the same picture. At the time, though, the story felt coherent.
That's one of the more unnerving things I've learned about the mind: a thought can be genuine without being true.

My brain wasn't lying to me deliberately. It was just doing what brains do; making sense of incomplete information. But I acted on the story. And that's where things became mine to own.


**Evidence vs. theory**

If I'd approached the situation the way I approach a security incident, I would have asked for evidence. What actually happened? What are the logs? What else could explain this? What would disprove the theory?
Instead, I believed the narrative.

Looking back, I can see how easily familiarity masquerades as evidence. When something resembles a pattern you've seen before, the brain gets rather enthusiastic about filling in the rest. It builds a coherent argument. It connects the dots. It even provides supporting evidence. What it doesn't always check is whether the dots belong together.

Kahneman's work has since given me language for something I'd lived without properly understanding. The mind's shortcuts, biases, and habit of constructing a coherent story from incomplete information. Not to excuse what happened. To understand it. There's an important difference between the two.


**The hardest part wasn't being wrong**

The hardest part was discovering that I had been wrong.
Once my thinking cleared, I saw the situation differently, and with it came an uncomfortable realisation: I had misunderstood someone's intentions.

There's a particular kind of guilt that follows that. Not the kind that comes with a mistake and passes quickly, but the kind that comes from revisiting the same event on a loop, with better information each time. My brain came with a replay button. Apparently I needed the director's cut.

For a long time, repetition felt like investigation. It wasn't. Repetition isn't corroboration. A thought becoming more familiar doesn't make it more accurate. A feeling getting stronger doesn't turn it into evidence. Certainty is not proof. But when you're living inside a story, those distinctions are remarkably easy to forget.


**Learning not to believe everything I think**

This is where Kahneman's book met another practice I'd been building — learning to observe my thoughts rather than automatically treating them as instructions.

I started asking different questions. What do I actually know? What am I assuming? What evidence am I ignoring? What other explanation fits the same facts? Am I mistaking something familiar for something true? And, less comfortably: what part of my own history or blind spots might be colouring this?

I've learned to catch my beliefs in the act. That doesn't mean I distrust my own mind. It means I no longer give it unrestricted administrative privileges. In security terms, we'd call that least privilege. Seems reasonable to apply the same principle internally.


**Understanding is not absolution**

There's another part of this that took longer to learn: understanding why I made the mistake doesn't remove responsibility for making it.

There are periods in life when our minds are under enough strain that judgement becomes distorted. That may explain an error. It doesn't erase what we do with it.

My actions had consequences, and I've never shied away from that. 

I was fortunate that the consequences of my mistake didn't materially harm someone else. But… I still had to live with having made it. There were stretches where guilt became its own kind of punishment. But punishment and accountability aren't the same thing. Accountability asks you to acknowledge what happened, understand your part in it, learn, and change how you behave. It doesn't require a life sentence of self-prosecution.
Eventually, forgiveness had to enter the picture too. Not the kind that quietly edits the past, and not the kind that says it didn't really matter. It did matter. The kind I mean says: 

*I understand how I got here. I know what I did. I've learned from it. I don't need to keep hurting myself to prove I take it seriously.*

Kahneman helped with that more than I expected. His work let me see my past self not as a different person, and not as someone beyond accountability, but as a mind doing what minds sometimes do, constructing a convincing explanation from imperfect information. Once I could see the machinery, I could stop treating the output as proof of some permanent flaw in myself.


**The new operating model**

I don't think the lesson is to distrust every thought. That's just a different trap. The lesson is to get curious about them.

When something feels obvious, I slow down. When something feels personal, I look for evidence. When I'm certain about someone's motives, I ask how I could possibly know. When an old pattern shows up, I check whether it actually belongs in the present. And when I catch myself building a neat little theory, I remember that neatness isn't the same thing as truth.

I still get things wrong. I probably always will. The difference is I now know my brain is capable of producing a remarkably persuasive argument without having all the facts, and that's made me more careful with judgement, more willing to question my assumptions, and, I hope, more generous in allowing other people the same complexity I now know exists inside my own head.

I don't need to forget what happened to move forward from it. I need to remember it accurately. I need to accept my part in it without making guilt a permanent address. And I need to keep questioning the stories my brain tells me, because sometimes the mind that produced the story wasn't entirely within my control.
What I did with that story still was.
