---
title: "I Was Kept in the Interview Process. I Forgot I Was Also Hiring."
description: "Dating, recruitment and the peculiar cruelty of being kept in the process without ever knowing whether there is a role at the end."
date: 2026-08-31
tags: ["Career", "Relationships", "Ambition", "Reflection"]
---
There is a particular kind of cruelty in not being rejected.

Rejection is, at least, efficient. Someone says no, you briefly question your life choices, perhaps listen to a disproportionately emotional playlist, and eventually redirect your energy elsewhere. Ambiguity is much harder to process. It keeps a tiny door open just wide enough for hope to squeeze through, while ensuring you can't quite close it yourself.

Which is perhaps why dating and recruitment have more in common than either industry would like to admit.

We had only been on two dates when we started joking that I was essentially being interviewed. There were references to auditions, next rounds, feedback and escalating concerns to HR. At one point, I accused the hiring manager of ghosting qualified candidates. He promised to raise a ticket.

We laughed.

In hindsight, the jokes had suspiciously good predictive accuracy.

Because while we were making fun of dysfunctional recruitment processes, I hadn't realised I was quietly being placed into an indefinite talent pool. There was no rejection, no offer and no clear indication of whether the position was actually open. Just intermittent communication explaining that the hiring manager had been exceptionally busy.

Now, I understand busy.

I'm not one of those people who interprets a delayed text message as evidence of kidnapping, emotional unavailability or a secret second family. People have jobs, deadlines, family obligations and occasionally buses that require more maintenance than a long-term relationship. Life gets complicated and sometimes the people we genuinely like don't receive the best version of our availability.

But there is a difference between being busy and keeping someone emotionally warm.

The latter feels remarkably like receiving an email that says, *Thank you for your continued interest. We are still assessing organisational requirements and will be in touch when we have greater clarity.*

Meanwhile, you notice the organisation is still actively hiring.

What fascinated me about the entire experience was that I wasn't actually waiting for an offer.

I was assessing him too.

I had questions about fit, aspirations and whether the chemistry of two dates could translate into something more substantial. I wasn't sitting by the phone refreshing my inbox, hoping to be selected from a shortlist. I simply assumed we were both figuring it out.

Somewhere along the way, however, the dynamic shifted. He became the organisation and I became the candidate. His uncertainty required my patience. His busyness explained the silence. His occasional reappearance was apparently sufficient evidence that the process remained active.

It was, in effect, an emotional non-compete clause.

I wasn't being hired, but it seemed preferable that I didn't join another organisation until the hiring manager had finished considering his options.

The irony is that I don't resent someone deciding I'm not the right fit. In fact, I respect it. I've interviewed for opportunities where, halfway through the conversation, I knew the role wasn't for me. Nothing was necessarily wrong with the organisation. The people were competent, the opportunity looked good and, on paper, it probably made perfect sense.

But fit is more complicated than that.

A good opportunity isn't automatically the right opportunity. You can be capable of doing a job without wanting to build your life around doing it. You can admire an organisation and still recognise that what they're looking for isn't what you're looking for.

That, to me, is the point of an interview.

It isn't a performance where one person desperately tries to convince the other to choose them. At its best, it's mutual due diligence. Both parties are asking essentially the same question: *Is this something worth investing in?*

Sometimes the answer is yes. Sometimes one person says no. Occasionally, everyone leaves the meeting wondering why they spent ninety minutes discussing their greatest weakness.

But nobody has failed simply because it wasn't a fit.

Which is perhaps why the ambiguity surrounding dating became more frustrating than the possibility of rejection itself.

I didn't need a board meeting, stakeholder consultation or a six-week strategic review. A simple, *“I enjoyed meeting you, but I don't think I want to pursue this further,”* would have been spectacular.

Excellent candidate experience. Five stars. Would recommend the organisation to others.

Instead, there is something oddly exhausting about being kept in a process without knowing whether there is actually a role at the end of it. Particularly when the occasional apology arrives, accompanied by another explanation of how busy life has been.

An apology without a change in behaviour is essentially an automated acknowledgement email.

*We have received your concern and appreciate your patience.*

I've also started to understand something about companies that conduct seven rounds of interviews for a single position. The panel interviews, the case studies, the personality assessments and the coffee with someone who isn't technically involved in the hiring decision but would *“really like to get a sense of your energy.”*

At some point, surely the company knows whether it wants to hire you. And if it doesn't, perhaps the problem isn't candidate assessment.

Perhaps it simply enjoys being wanted.

There is an ego boost in keeping options open. Someone remains interested. They might say yes. They haven't gone anywhere yet. You don't have enough conviction to choose them, but you also don't have enough conviction to lose them.

I suspect this is where dating and corporate recruitment finally converge: sometimes people confuse having options with having a strategy.

So, I've decided I'm not checking in for the next round of interviews.

Not dramatically. There will be no exit interview, strongly worded Glassdoor review or passive-aggressive LinkedIn post about organisational culture. I'm simply going to assume the role is no longer aligned with my aspirations and continue exploring other opportunities.

Which, as it happens, is exactly what I'm doing.

This week, I have an interview for an Associate Director role. It is ambitious, and I may well be punching above my weight. They may decide I'm not the right fit. I may walk away and decide they're not the right fit either.

And that's the distinction I had temporarily forgotten.

I'm not walking into that conversation as a candidate waiting to be chosen. I'm walking in on equal footing. They have something I might want, and I have something they might need. We get to find out whether those things belong together. No one is doing anyone a favour by showing up.

Ideally, if either party decides it isn't a fit, we communicate that before scheduling another six rounds and a personality assessment.

But perhaps the bigger lesson here isn't about him, or even about interviewing. It's about what I refuse to let this experience take from me.

I don't want one person's ambivalence to make me cynical about possibility. I don't want two dates and an impressive amount of inconsistent follow-through to become evidence that connection isn't worth the risk. And I certainly don't want to become so determined to avoid disappointment that I stop being curious about people, opportunities and rooms where I might not yet know whether I belong.

That would be giving far too much significance to someone who, ultimately, just couldn't make up his mind.

The best outcome isn't finding the perfect explanation for his behaviour. It isn't receiving a final apology polished enough to make the waiting feel worthwhile, and it isn't even getting the offer.

The best outcome is to keep going.

To continue testing myself in the market, walking into rooms that intimidate me a little and meeting people without assuming every interesting conversation has to lead somewhere. To remain ambitious without becoming attached to every opportunity, and optimistic without confusing optimism with obligation.

I still want bigger challenges, more interesting conversations and opportunities that surprise me. I still want to meet people who surprise me too. I still want to take chances on things that might not work out.

The difference is that I want to become more discerning about where I place my energy.

Not less open. Not less optimistic. Just better at recognising the difference between being genuinely considered and simply being kept in the process. Because the goal was never to get every offer. It was to find the right ones.

And fortunately, the market is still open.
