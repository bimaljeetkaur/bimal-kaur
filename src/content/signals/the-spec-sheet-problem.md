I recently applied for a Cyber Security Engineer role. On paper, I matched almost everything in the requirements — except one thing: years of experience. My actual number was lower than what was listed.

I got a call. Good conversation. And then the answer came back, relayed politely by the recruiter: the client wanted someone from a purely SOC engineering background. My experience, which includes actively enabling a SOC engineering team, building the scaffolding other engineers operate inside, didn't fit the shape of the box they'd drawn.

To be fair to the recruiter here: they weren't the one who wrote that spec. They're matching a brief, not making a judgment call on what transfers and what doesn't. That's a hiring manager's line in the sand, relayed faithfully. No villain in this story, just a narrow filter doing exactly what narrow filters do.

But it got me thinking about the "years on paper" problem more broadly. Is a slow-burn five years in a role that never really stretched you worth more than eighteen intense months of actually building things, breaking them, and rebuilding them properly? Time served and skill compounded aren't the same currency, even though most resumes are forced to report them as if they are. A number on a spec sheet measures presence. It doesn't measure what someone actually did with the time.

Which Brings Me to the One Thing I Have Been Consistent About

While the job search plays out, there's one place I've shown up on schedule, every time: my detection engineering lab.

My laptop is a Dell Inspiron 5558. It is not, by any modern standard, a lab machine. Getting it there has meant dealing with the unglamorous parts first. Sorting out a failing hard drive with bad sectors, planning a proper SSD swap, working out storage partitions like a puzzle instead of a purchase. None of that makes for an impressive line on a CV. But it's the version of "dedication" nobody puts in a cover letter: the part where you fix the machine before you can even start fixing the problem it's meant to help you solve.

On top of that, I've been working through the real chain, threat modeling → threat use cases → Sigma rules → SPL correlation searches in Splunk. I picked three MITRE ATT&CK focus areas deliberately: Persistence, Exfiltration, Lateral Movement, rather than trying to boil the ocean. I've been deliberate about sequencing too, holding off on installing the Windows evaluation ISO until I'm actually ready to use its 90-day clock properly, rather than burning it prematurely out of impatience.

No one's checking in on this. No recruiter is grading it against a spec sheet. That's exactly why it counts. It's the one thread in this whole story that isn't dependent on someone else's brief, or someone else's definition of "enough experience."

A recruiter relaying a narrow spec isn't a villain. They're doing their job, passing on someone else's line in the sand. But the lab is entirely mine. That's the one commitment that was never contingent on anyone else's brief, or anyone else's checkbox.

If there's a throughline here, it's this: good intentions don't compound, and neither does a headcount of years on a page. Hours do. The deliberate, unglamorous, "no one's watching" kind. The spec sheet is a fair call, made by people playing their own hand as best they could. 
The lab is the one place where the outcome was always entirely up to me. So that's where I've put the work.
